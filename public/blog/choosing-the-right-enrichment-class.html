How to Choose the Right Enrichment Class for Your Child's Grade Level

A practical guide to matching subject, format, and pace to where your child actually is.

With so many enrichment options available — math, science, coding, debate, test prep — it's easy to pick a class based on the title alone and hope it fits. A few simple checks upfront save a lot of frustration later.

Start with where your child is, not their grade number

Grade level is a starting point, not the whole answer. A 7th grader who's breezed through pre-algebra needs a different math class than a 7th grader still building confidence with fractions. Before enrolling, look at the class description for prerequisites or a stated skill level, and when in doubt, ask the instructor directly — a five-minute conversation beats a guess.

Match the format to your family's actual schedule

Live online classes are easier to fit around sports, siblings, and unpredictable weeks. In-person classes add a commute but often build stronger peer connection and hands-on work (think robotics kits or lab experiments). Neither is "better" — the right choice is whichever one your family will actually show up to consistently, since consistency matters more than format for an enrichment class to pay off.

Check class size before you check the price

A lower price on a much larger class often costs more in the long run if your child needs individual attention to stay engaged. Smaller classes mean the instructor can actually adapt to how your child learns — ask about capacity before comparing prices side by side.

Let interest lead for enrichment, let need lead for test prep

For pure enrichment (coding, debate, public speaking), following genuine curiosity produces better outcomes than following a resume-building instinct — kids stick with what they enjoy. For test prep specifically, timing matters more than interest; see our companion guide on when to start SAT/ACT prep.

Give it three sessions before deciding

The first class can be awkward for any kid in a new setting. Before switching classes or concluding "this isn't for them," give it three sessions — that's usually enough for a child to settle in and for you to see whether the fit is actually wrong, versus just new.
